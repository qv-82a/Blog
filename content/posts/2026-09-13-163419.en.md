# Business Administration Learned from Failure: Anatomy of the J.League Stadium Issue (Part 1)

## Chapter 1: Any Business Funded by Other People’s Money Inevitably Corrupts

"Revitalizing local communities through professional sports."

"Delivering dreams and inspiration to citizens."

Behind such appealing slogans lies an ongoing issue involving local municipalities nationwide: the construction of professional soccer stadiums. With massive construction costs ranging from 10 to tens of billions of yen, coupled with annual subsidies required for post-completion operation and maintenance, public criticism is intensifying against the fact that a significant portion of these expenditures relies on public funds (taxpayer money).

Why has such substantial public funding become a baseline premise for professional sports—an industry that is fundamentally a private sector entertainment and commercial business?

To state the conclusion upfront: it stems from a structural moral hazard where commercially unviable projects are rendered viable by transferring credit risk onto municipal governments.

### 1. Projects Rejected by Banks as "Unbankable"

When a private enterprise undertakes an investment on the scale of hundreds of millions or billions of yen, its first recourse is to financial institutions. Banks rigorously audit business plans to dispassionately determine whether capital can be reliably recovered.

What happens when a stadium construction project is subjected to these credit evaluation standards?

- **Fatal Deficiency in Cash Flow**
  
  Hosting approximately 20 home games per year offers virtually no prospect of recovering tens of billions of yen in capital expenditure (CAPEX) alongside annual operational and maintenance costs (OPEX).

- **Absence of Collateral Value**
  
  Single-purpose soccer stadiums possess extremely low asset versatility. In the event of default, it is exceptionally difficult for a lender to foreclose, liquidate, or repurpose the facility.

Under professional financial scrutiny, such proposals are summarily rejected as "unbankable" due to lack of commercial viability.

Ordinarily, this would mark the failure of the project, forcing proponents to either drastically downscale the scope or independently secure private equity and sponsorships.

However, market discipline fails to operate here. Instead, a sophisticated financial maneuver is deployed: shifting the risk that banks refuse to bear directly onto the municipality (and by extension, future taxpayers).

### 2. "Municipal Credit" as an Unlimited Wallet

While banks will not lend a single yen against a club's standalone commercial viability, they will readily extend credit based on a municipality's debt capacity (backed by future tax revenues).

The local government acts as the primary borrower, issuing municipal bonds to finance construction. Consequently, economically unfeasible projects that would otherwise be eliminated by market forces are given the illusion of viability and greenlit for construction.

| **Comparison Metric**           | **Standard Private Business**                                   | **Publicly Dependent Stadium Construction**                                               |
|:------------------------------- |:--------------------------------------------------------------- |:----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Capital Financing**           | Commercial bank loans / Equity investment (Strict underwriting) | Municipal bonds / Government subsidies / Public funds                                     |
| **Rigors of Business Planning** | Precise projections of profitability and payback period         | Qualitative justifications (e.g., "¥XX billion in economic impact," "community vibrancy") |
| **Risk in Case of Failure**     | Corporate insolvency / Private accountability                   | Annual operational deficits offset by citizens via taxes                                  |

Private-sector managers engage in rigorous financial modeling and risk mitigation precisely because they face the risk of bankruptcy upon failure.

Conversely, municipal officials and local government heads operate over a safety net: local governments do not go bankrupt over operating deficits (in the worst-case scenario, they can simply raise taxes or cut public welfare services). Because they are not risking their own capital, they casually acquire low-yield, high-cost assets that no private entity would ever purchase.

### 3. Economic Simulations: "Not Technically False," but "Nobody Held Accountable"

When justifying public investment, administrators and management consultants invariably submit thick report packages claiming "An Economic Impact of ¥XX Billion!"

However, this involves an intentional conceptual substitution:

- **Operating Revenue (Cash Flow):** Real monetary inflows generated directly by the stadium **(In reality, a severe deficit)**.

- **Economic Impact (Estimated):** Indirect estimates based on secondary spending, such as beer consumed by spectators in the city or local cement procurement **(No guarantee of direct recovery as tax revenue)**.

Administrators present simulations to the municipal council based strictly on best-case scenarios—assuming sold-out crowds and high per-capita spending.

Post-completion, even when actual performance falls far short of targets and millions to tens of millions of dollars in annual management subsidies (deficit coverage) drain public coffers, **neither the consultants who modeled the plan, the leadership who approved it, nor the council members who voted for it bear personal financial liability or institutional accountability.**

The institutional framework allows key decision-makers to transition to subsequent roles or complete their terms under the defense that it was merely a "forecasting error."

### Conclusion: The Ultimate Fate of Businesses Built on Other People’s Money

Fundamentally, public expenditure that disregards financial profitability should be reserved strictly for essential public goods—such as roads, water utilities, fire services, and public welfare—that are vital to citizens' survival yet impossible for the private sector to supply.

Subsidizing commercial venues for specific private enterprises—facilities where general public access is typically restricted to preserve the turf—under the guise of "public works" constitutes a clear violation of market principles.

No enterprise built on external dependence and devoid of risk-taking can develop sustainably.
